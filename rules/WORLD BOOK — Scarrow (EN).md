# SCARROW

## World Book

*English edition. Every name in this book is new: the game, the crystal,
the land, the factions, the people. The rules do not change by a single
word — only what things are called in the world.*

---

This book is not rules. There is no number in it you need to remember at
the table. It explains why the ground under your tokens glows green, who
sits across from you, and why they have exactly the same right to this
field as you do.

**The short version.** Two hundred and twelve years ago a walking machine
the size of a city cut a furrow across the continent, down to where the
rock is hot — and stopped. The land closed the wound with crystal. That
crystal, **cicatrine**, is the heat of a dead nation locked in stone. The
wound healed badly: the great scar, called **the Weal**, still breaks open
in **weeps**, and each weep bleeds crystal for a few months. Four peoples
grown from the ruins of that nation share every new weep under the
**Tally Accord**, which turns a war into a season with a score. Cutting
crystal out of a living weep while everyone else is trying to do the
same is called **scarrowing**. You command one of four crews at the weep
by Magpie Ford — in the year the dead machine took its first step in two
centuries.

---

## 1. The Holdmark and the Harrow

Nobody calls the vanished country by its old name. People call it **the
Holdmark**, the way it called itself, and they say "back in the Holdmark"
the way old people say "before the war".

The Holdmark was huge: cold in the north, rich in grain in the south,
and short of energy everywhere. Two and a half centuries ago it solved
that hunger with one project, and everything you will see on the board
still depends on it.

The project was the **Deep Traverse Engine**. Everyone called it **the
Harrow**.

The Harrow was a walking machine more than three kilometres long: forty-two
legs, six storeys in each, living decks for eleven thousand people,
schools, a hospital, a greenhouse under a glass roof, and a choir that
sang from the bridge on holidays. It did not roll — it walked, nine metres
a stride, three strides an hour — and in front of it, across the full
width of its hull, it cut the ground. Not dug: cut. At its front ran the
**share-arc**, an edge heated white, and behind it lay a furrow two and a
half kilometres deep, down to where the rock is warm on its own.

The Holdmark took its heat from the furrow. Along both lips it laid the
**heatline** — pipes that carried deep heat to the cities — and beside
the heatline a service railway, the **Weal Line**. The Harrow walked east
for thirty-one years. Behind it lay nine hundred and twelve kilometres of
glowing trench, and on that trench the Holdmark warmed itself, melted
its metal, dried its grain and lit its windows.

There were songs about the Harrow. *"The furrow: bread and warmth for a
thousand years"* was stamped on the coins. Its crew were counted a
second people: children were born aboard, couples married aboard, some
never set foot on the ground in their lives. Every schoolroom had a map
where the furrow ran in a red line across the whole continent to the
Eastern Sea, and the teacher would point: here is the Harrow now, and
here is where it will be when you are grown.

It never got there.

## 2. The Halt

Day eleven thousand three hundred and two. Early morning. The Harrow
stands on the heights above the river Magpie, forty kilometres from where
your Command Centre stands today.

The last thing it sent over **the Call** — the Holdmark's service radio
net, on which it reported to the capital every four hours — was:

> "Traverse One, sixth watch report. Halted. Share running over
> tolerance, beneath us —"

In the capital's receiving log someone wrote in pencil: *"signal lost"*.

Nobody knows what was beneath them. The crew walked off the Harrow nine
days later, and by then there was no time left to ask.

In those nine days the furrow filled.

From the floor, the walls, the lips, along its whole length — all nine
hundred and twelve kilometres at once — the crystal came. Green,
lit from within, faceted and dense, it rose from below at a speed
witnesses compared to boiling, and by the ninth day it stood level with
the ground. Where the glowing trench had been lay a lumpy green ridge
knee-high, crossed every few metres by ropy folds, exactly like the drawn
edges of a wound. It was named for what it looked like: **the Weal**.

The heatline went cold in a single night. The crystal took all the heat
of the furrow — not quenched it, took it. The pipes along the Weal stood
cold, and the Weal itself was warm to the touch and glowed.

The Holdmark lasted three winters without heat.

No one conquered it and nothing blew it up. First the foundries stopped:
they had nothing to melt with. Then the northern cities froze. Then the
south stopped selling grain to the north, because the north had stopped
paying. And in the third year came **the Drowning**: the Weal, growing
downward, shifted the land beneath it, the western coast sank eighteen
metres, and the sea walked into the capital. **Vesk**, the Holdmark's
capital on the Estuary Flats, went under in one autumn week — all of it
but the rooftops.

Years are counted from the Halt. On the board it is **212 A.H.** — two
hundred and twelve years After the Halt.

## 3. Cicatrine

### What it is

The crystal that filled the furrow is the Holdmark's heat, locked in
stone.

This is not a metaphor. That is how the substance behaves: it grows where
the crust is opened down to heat, grows faster the hotter the wound, draws
the heat into itself and holds it in its lattice. The land did not
"release" the crystal or "give birth" to it. The land **scarred over**: it
closed a deep cut the way a body closes one, with a thick seam of tissue
denser and coarser than what was there before.

The name came from the doctors. When the field surgeons of the first
years saw the Weal, they said one word: *cicatrix* — the old medical
term for scar tissue. Miners bent it into the shape of a mineral name,
**cicatrine**, and within ten years nobody remembered it had been a
diagnosis.

At the table everyone just says **trine**. Soldiers call it **embers**,
for the light and the warmth. Foundry workers call it "scar". Old people
call it "the Holdmark's warmth", and they are not joking.

### Why people fight over it

Cicatrine gives back its locked heat — slowly, evenly, for years. A piece
the size of a fist heats a cottage for a winter. A crate keeps a foundry
running for a week. Since the Halt it is the only heat on the continent
that does not have to be fetched from under two and a half kilometres of
rock: it lies on the surface and glows. Everything that works in 212
A.H. works on trine.

But the important discovery came in 40 A.H., and it came by accident.

### Knitsteel

In the northern foundry towns people tried melting crystal in with iron,
just to keep the metal hot longer. The result was a new alloy — dull,
greenish at the break, heavy. A week later a foundry hand named **Prue
Garner** noticed that a cracked ingot of it, left by the furnace, had
**knitted** — not all of it, only where the furnace generator's cable
touched it.

The alloy of cicatrine and metal is called **knitsteel**. It does to
metal what the crystal did to the land: it scars over. While current
runs through knitsteel, it closes holes, cracks and cuts — not
instantly, but while you watch. Take the current away, and it is just
heavy metal that can be holed like any other.

Everything on your board is made of knitsteel: barracks, factories,
extractors, vehicle hulls, infantry armour, tower frames. That is why
**energy on the board is never spent, only moved**: it is the current
that keeps knitsteel alive. That is why a hit on a building **knocks
energy out of it** — the breach cuts the circuit and the current runs
back to its source. And that is why **an energy cube placed in a damaged
slot removes the damage**: the current is back, and the scar closes the
hole. Engineers of all four crews call it "feeding" a building. Medics
who do the same to a wounded soldier's armour call it the same thing.

### What it costs

Cicatrine is not poisonous, but its dust settles in the lungs and scars
there too. This is **weal-cough**. Foundry workers get it by forty,
extractor crews by thirty, and those who break open storage under fire —
whenever. Crystal broken across the grain gives up all its heat at once:
a flash, green flame, a crater. That is why **a destroyed storage burns
along with what was inside** — nobody captures a trine store; they lose
it.

## 4. Weeps

The Weal healed badly.

From 60 A.H. it began to **weep** — the surgeon's word for a wound that
closed on top and keeps festering underneath, breaking out to the
surface somewhere away from the cut. Tens, sometimes a hundred kilometres
from the Weal the ground splits, heat comes out of the crack, and
crystal starts growing into it the way it once grew into the furrow. It
grows fast — within a week the ground bristles with man-high crystal
ridges — and it can be cut, and it grows back.

Every weep lives the same life:

- **Opening.** The ground bursts, warm steam pours out of the cracks, the
  horizon glows at night. Anyone listening to the Call hears about it the
  same day.
- **Season.** For a few months the weep runs. Crystal cut from the
  **wellheads** — the hottest spots, where the crack reaches the surface —
  grows back. Your whole game happens here.
- **Crusting.** The heat fades, crystal stops growing back, the cracks
  skin over with grey crust, and a year later the land is dead. A weep
  never reopens.

Cartographers write "focus" and "wellhead" on their maps. Soldiers keep it
simple: **running** means we fight; **crusted** means we count.

Nobody knows where the next weep will open. There have been forty-seven.
Forty-six have crusted. The forty-seventh is yours.

## 5. The land

The Weal cuts the continent in two. The land on both sides of it, a
hundred kilometres each way, is **the Wealands**. Everything else is just
"out there".

- **The Weal** — nine hundred and twelve kilometres of green scar, from
  the Estuary Flats in the west to the dead Harrow in the east. You can
  walk on it but not live on it: too warm and too bright — cattle go
  blind, people stop sleeping.
- **The Head** — the eastern end of the Weal, where the Harrow stands.
  Three kilometres of knitsteel on a rise above the Magpie; knitsteel
  without current takes two hundred years to start rusting. The share-arc
  is still warm. Nobody settles near the Head.
- **Vesk** — the drowned capital in the west. Above the bay they call
  **the Drown** stand rooftops, spires and the top floors of ministries.
  People live on the roofs.
- **The Forgelands** — the north, the Holdmark's old foundry towns. Cold,
  smoky, crowded. Today this is **the Smelt**.
- **The Dryfold** — the south, the old breadbasket. As the Weal grew it
  drank the rivers: crystal needs water to grow, and every river that
  crossed the furrow drained into it. The south dried to ochre. Today
  this is **the Fold**.
- **Calder** — a city halfway between the north and the Weal, burned in
  118 A.H. Where it stood there is now **scab** — the dead crust of a
  crusted weep. Its people are called **the Ashborn**.

### What you see on the board

Every tile on your board has a name, and the locals know it.

| on the board | what it is called | what it is |
|---|---|---|
| waste, hills, rocks | **the dry** | the parched land of the Dryfold and the Wealands |
| spawn tile | **wellhead**; soldiers say "tap" | the crack of a weep where crystal grows |
| impassable hex | **scab** | the crust of an old weep: nothing passes, nothing builds |
| chasm | **sink** | an underground branch of the Weal that fell through to the surface |
| magma | **bleed** | where the Weal never closed and still gives off heat |
| water | **drown** | sunken, flooded land; Holdmark streets under the water |
| radiation | **kettles** | abandoned Holdmark power plants that heated its cities before the Harrow |
| yellow-green sludge | **gall** | groundwater leached by the scar; murky and caustic |
| storm | **dry storm** | lightning without rain: over a fresh weep the crystal charges the air |
| fire | **burn** | burning dry land; set off by bleeds and by battle |
| neutral structure | **cairn** | a service post of the Harrow, see below |
| container on the board | **drop ring** | a Provender landing pad, see below |

## 6. How a weep is shared

### The Great Weep

For the first sixty years weeps were shared however it went: whoever got
there first cut crystal until the others arrived. The others always
arrived.

By 118 A.H. four powers had formed on the continent, described below, and
all four came to the thirty-first weep, which opened by Calder — a large
city that belonged to no one yet. That season is called **the Great
Weep**. It lasted eight months instead of three, because a weep that is
cut without pause runs longer — and in those eight months each side threw
in everything it had.

Calder stood at the edge of the wellheads. Nobody meant to burn it. It
was burned one quarter at a time — each time a quarter held an enemy
headquarters. The last quarter, Bell Street, was to be shelled by the
Smelt's foundry mortars on the night of the twelfth of November.

### The Refusal

The mortar battery commander, **Tomas Rell**, twenty-six years old,
received the order, looked through his binoculars at Bell Street, where
about four hundred people sat in the cellars, and said: "No."

He pulled the breech blocks from his guns, put them in a crate, sat on
the crate and stayed there until morning, when they came for him. His
crew — nine people — sat down beside him.

The Smelt tried Rell for refusing an order and sent him to the foundry
for life. Bell Street survived; all four hundred walked out in the
morning. And three days later the commanders of all four sides, each in
their own headquarters, each with the same thought — *the next Rell
could be my gunner, and the next Calder could be my city* — stopped
firing. Nobody negotiated. They just stopped.

This is called **the Refusal**. Three years later, in 121 A.H., the Refusal
became a treaty.

### The Tally Accord

It was signed on the only neutral ground on the continent: in the staff
car of **the Ledger** — the train that runs on the Weal Line — at the
Fortieth Mile siding. Three sides signed: the Smelt, the Fold and the
Chancery of Vesk. Ever since, the sides are called **crews**, after the
work crews the Accord licenses to cut a weep.

The document is the **Tally Accord**, usually just **the Tally**. It is
short, and every officer in the Wealands knows it by heart. Here it is,
without the preamble:

> **Article One.** An open weep belongs to no one. It is open to every
> crew that signed the Tally, from opening to crusting.
>
> **Article Two.** Crews come to a weep on their own feet and with their
> own hearth. Where a crew's hearth stands, there stands its right.
>
> **Article Three, Rell's Article.** No one touches another crew's hearth.
> A crew that decides otherwise breaks its seal before the tallyman, and
> from that hour it is within its rights — and every other crew is within
> its rights against it. A hearth razed under a broken seal ends that
> crew's season: the weep passes to the breaker.
>
> **Article Four.** The season is scored by the Ledger's tallyman. Every
> deed a crew proves to the tallyman is written in the tally book.
>
> **Article Five.** At crusting the count is closed. The crews' place in
> the Ledger's queue for the coming year follows the tally book.
>
> **Article Six.** The Ledger, its people and its cars are not to be
> touched.
>
> **Article Seven.** What falls from the sky belongs to whoever opens it.
>
> **Article Eight.** The Harrow's cairns belong to no one.

Everything that happens at your table rests on these eight articles.

### The hearth and the seal

**The hearth** is your Command Centre. The word comes from the Harrow,
where the command section with the main heat core was called that. Each
crew has its own: a prefabricated command post delivered to the weep in
one piece, with a cicatrine heat core. It is both a power source and a
consumer. While it stands, you are on the weep. Once it is razed, you are
nobody there.

**The seal** is the combat lock module. Every crew's hearth carries a
Ledger seal — a lead stamp of the tallyman's on the wire leading to its
weapons. To break it is to announce that you no longer play by Rell's
Article. In the Wealands this is called "breaking Rell", and nobody says
it with approval. But the Accord allows it, which means someone will
always try.

### Stars

The Ledger's tallymen keep the book in an old clerk's hand: each proven
deed gets a line, and next to it an **asterisk** — a footnote mark
pointing to the inspection record. By the end of the season each crew
has a column of them. Those are what get counted.

That is where "stars" — victory points — come from. There is no glory in
them. A star in the tally book is a place in the Ledger's queue for the
whole next year, and a place in that queue is the only way to sell trine
into the cities. Trine without a queue place is just a warm rock.

### Charges

A commander on the weep does not decide alone what their crew wants. Home
sends them **charges** — what a house demands of its people in the field:
take the high ground, hold the extractors to the end of the month, ship
out so much crystal. A charge fulfilled and proven to the tallyman is a
line in the book and a star. And there is more: for a fulfilled charge,
home sends help — that is the part of a mission reward that grants an
extra action.

A **great charge** — a super-mission — is written not by headquarters but
by the house itself, and people talk about it for years.

## 7. The Ledger

The Ledger is a train, and it is the only institution of the Holdmark that
outlived the Holdmark.

It ran on the Weal Line long before the Halt, carrying the Harrow its
spare parts, food and relief crews, and carrying reports back. After the
Halt the train stayed on the rails because it had nowhere to go off them.
Forty cars, eleven of them armoured, three trine-fired locomotives, eight
hundred crew who have lived in the cars ever since — **ledgerfolk**. They
are born on the train and they die on it. They are commanded by the
**trainmaster**.

The Ledger does three things.

**It trades.** The Weal Line is the only overland road linking the north,
the south, the Drown and the Head. Everything that moves from one region
to another moves by Ledger. Once a round the Ledger pulls up by the field,
and two cars open beside the track — those are the two offers of the
market card. You pay in **chits** — the train's tokens, the only money
all four crews accept; in the rules they are coins. If you want the train
to uncouple those cars and bring others, you pay in trine: the engines
need fuel to fetch a new set. That is refreshing the market card.

**It counts.** In the staff car ride the **tallymen** — the Accord's
inspectors. They step out at every weep, declare the opening, walk the
field with clipboards and keep the tally book. A tallyman carries no
weapon, and nobody shoots at one: Article Six. Tallymen are trained on
the train from the age of eight, and the first thing they are taught is
to have no opinion.

**It keeps quiet.** The ledgerfolk are the richest people on the
continent, and everyone knows it. They sell every crew what it will fight
the others with, and they buy from the winner what the winner took from
the losers. The Ledger profits from a season that never ends. The
ledgerfolk deny this, and so far nobody has proved otherwise.

## 8. What falls from the sky

### The Provender

The Holdmark had a supply system that needed no people. It was called
**the Provender**: a web of unmanned cargo airships hanging in the upper
sky, and depots on the ground that filled them. The Provender worked out
by itself who needed what, and dropped cargo by parachute onto **drop
rings** — round concrete landing pads laid all along the Weal Line and
around every Harrow work site.

The Holdmark has been gone for two hundred years. The Provender still
works.

No one can switch it off, because no one knows where it is controlled
from. It keeps doing what it always did: looking for **hot work sites** on
the ground and supplying them. There are two kinds of hot site on the
continent now — the Head and the weeps. So about a week after a weep
opens, crates start falling on it: parachute crates with harness straps
and Holdmark markings, landing on whatever old drop rings happen to be
nearby.

These crates are called **foundlings**. What is inside cannot be guessed:
the Provender packs by its own two-hundred-year-old tables. Tools,
rations, modules, parts, and sometimes things nobody can identify.
Article Seven: a foundling belongs to whoever opens it — which is why
everyone runs for the rings.

Containers are printed on the board where the drop rings lie. That is not
the artist's whim; that is the Provender's geography.

### The scrapyard

Whatever is shot down on the field gets dragged home. A wrecked enemy
token is knitsteel, and knitsteel is always a trophy: from its wreckage
come **cogs** — assemblies that can be taken apart, understood and copied.
Cogs are printed on the back of almost every token; that is what your
mechanics find when they tear the wreck apart in the **scrapyard** by your
headquarters. Sometimes they find something else in the wreck: every crew
carries a foundling or two in the hold.

## 9. Cairns and the Call

### Cairns

As the Harrow walked, it left **cairns** behind — service posts it cast
itself, printing them from knitsteel on the move, in layers, with no
seams and no windows. Smooth, pale grey, half sunk into the ground, they
stand along the Weal and around every old Harrow camp. Small cairns were
switchboard posts; large ones were heatline watchmen's huts, with room
for infantry inside.

Cairns are dead until there is current in them. Put energy on the circle
stamped on the threshold, and green lights up in the cracks: the
knitsteel wakes and remembers it is a post. The Tally says cairns belong
to no one. That means anyone may stand in them — and anyone may throw
them out.

### The Call

No radio in the Wealands reaches past five kilometres: the heat of the
weeps and the dry storms drown the air. Only one thing works — **the
Call**, the Holdmark's service net, with masts along the Weal Line. It was
built so the Harrow could report to the capital, and it was built to
last: the masts stand, the trine-powered relays hum.

The Call has **four channels**, one for each service of the Harrow.
Commanders give orders only through it — there is no other way. And the
channels are named as they were named on the Harrow:

- **Deploy** — the track service: where to stand, what to put up.
- **Acquire** — the supply service: what to bring in, what to buy.
- **Control** — the power service and the watch: current and fire.
- **Research** — the survey service and the lab: what to find, what to
  understand.

Every crew on a weep sits on the same four channels, and in each circle
the whole Call goes on air in turn. If your order goes out on a channel
another crew has already taken this circle, the channel is jammed and
only half reaches your people: one action instead of two. That is why
orders are chosen in silence and revealed in turn.

**Lie Low** is radio silence. You go out on no channel, and your people
act on standing orders as best they can. A silent crew frightens everyone
more than a loud one.

## 10. The four crews

Each of the four crews is heir to a piece of the Holdmark. Each has its
own truth about cicatrine and its own old grudge. Each sits under the
Tally because without it things would be worse, and each secretly thinks
the Tally was written against it.

---

### THE SMELT — blue crew

*"We don't fight for a weep. We process it."*

**Who they are.** The Forgelands, the north. Twelve foundry towns that
survived the three winters after the Halt because they were first to fire
their furnaces with crystal, and in 40 A.H. they discovered knitsteel.
The whole continent has stood on their alloy ever since. The Smelt is not
a state and not a company; it is the **Council of Heats**: one delegate
from each foundry, chaired by the **chief metallurgist**. Decisions follow
output: more heats, more votes.

**What they believe.** Cicatrine is ore. A weep is a deposit. Anything
beyond that is poetry, and the Smelt leaves poetry to the south. The Smelt
was first to keep records of the weeps and has the best maps of the
Wealands. The Smelt respects the Tally, because the Tally is order, and
order lowers costs.

**How they fight.** Numbers and schedules. Their factories are cheap and
go up in a shift; vehicles roll off them in pairs — thin-walled machines
the south calls **tins**: nobody mourns them, there are many, and they
get melted down again from their own wrecks. Smelt infantry are
**screen rifles** with anti-air guns: since the Night of Keys the
foundry folk fear the sky most of all. The Smelt's pride are the
**flatirons**, heavy transport gunships, slow as a barge and just as hard
to sink. Smelt towers are foundry mortars that hit structures. The same
ones that fired on Calder. The Smelt never renamed them.

**What they call things.** Hearth — "the office". Wellhead — "deposit
number such-and-such". Energy — "current". Scrapyard — "the charge bin".
Tally book — "the accounts".

**What they want.** Quota. The Smelt feeds the north, and the north is
cold.

**What they fear.** What the late Ilan Stane wrote in his notebooks.

**Faces.**

**Vera Stane**, 58, chief metallurgist of the Council of Heats. Grew up at
the furnace, forty years in the foundry, weal-cough since thirty-nine.
Runs the Council with an iron hand and a spreadsheet. Fifteen years ago
her husband, hydrologist **Ilan Stane**, presented the Council a paper
*On the Spread*: by his figures, a weep that is cut without pause runs
longer, and every weep that crusts lengthens the Weal's underground
sinks, until one day the sinks will join — and the ground between them
will fall in. The Council rejected the paper. Vera voted against. A year
later Ilan died of weal-cough. The paper lies in her safe. She rereads it
every winter, and every winter she convinces herself the figures are
wrong.

**Kessa Stane**, 31, their daughter, head of the Smelt expedition at the
Magpie Ford weep. The first Smelt expedition head who did not grow up at
the furnace but trained as a cartographer. In spring, clearing out her
father's cottage, she found the drafts of the paper — not in the safe,
but in the cellar, in a pickle jar. She brought them to the weep. She
needs just three measurements at a wellhead to learn whether her father
was right.

**Gord Tallow**, 44, stoker at Foundry Four, flatiron driver. Organiser of
the **Dust Strike** two years ago, when the foundries stood idle for nine
days: the workers demanded the Council pay for the cough. The Council paid
half. Tallow wasn't jailed — he was sent to the weep. He knows what that
means. He is a good driver.

---

### THE ASHBORN — red crew

*"We have no land. So the sky is ours."*

**Who they are.** Descendants of the people of Calder, burned in the Great
Weep. When the weep under Calder crusted, the city became scab — nothing
to rebuild, nothing to bury. Forty thousand people went south to the
Fold, which fed them for twelve years. Then they left there too — for
the sky.

The Ashborn live on **roving airfields**: a strip, canvas hangars, a
workshop, a kitchen. A month later everything is packed up and flown
elsewhere. An airfield goes up in a day — the one thing they build faster
than anyone. They have had no homes for almost a hundred years, and they
say they've got used to it. That's not true.

**What they believe.** That a weep is not a deposit and not a harvest, but
the thing that burned their city. That the land should not belong to
those who come to cut it. That the Tally is a pact of Calder's killers,
signed without Calder. The Ashborn were not invited to the Tally: in 121
nobody counted them as a side.

**The Night of Keys.** In 152 A.H., after twenty years of raids on the
extractors of all three crews, sixty Ashborn crews landed at night on the
roofs of the Ledger's cars as the train stood at a siding — and in the
morning the trainmaster found a bundle of keys on his desk. Keys to the
flats of Calder, which every Ashborn family has kept since 118. There was
no note. None was needed. A week later the Tally had a fourth crew. Only
one delegate voted against: the Chancery of Vesk's.

**How they fight.** With the sky and the ram. Their airbases are cheap,
aircraft come off them in pairs — light bombers that hunt vehicles.
People are few among the Ashborn and barracks are expensive for them —
but every soldier and every machine goes after structures: **rammers**,
sappers, bulldozers with blades. An Ashborn does not hold the high ground.
An Ashborn tears down whatever is built on it. Ashborn gear is tough:
whoever grew up on a roving airfield can take a beating. Their towers
hit infantry.

**Names.** The Ashborn have no surnames. Each takes the name of the
street where their family's house stood in Calder: **Mara of Bell
Street**, **Ulla of Flour Street**, **Stefan of Forge Street**. Every
family has the key to that house, and it hangs in the cockpit above the
instrument panel. The key passes to the eldest child the first time they
land a plane alone.

**What they call things.** Hearth — "the stove". It is the only stove they
have. Wellhead — "the weep", and nothing else. Energy — "heat". Tally
book — "their book".

**What they want.** The Ashborn argue about this, and the argument may
split the crew.

**What they fear.** That nobody remembers Calder anymore.

**Faces.**

**Mara of Bell Street**, 99. Born in Calder in 113 A.H.; she was five,
sitting in a Bell Street cellar on the night of the Refusal, and walked
out alive in the morning. The last living person born in Calder. In 152,
a forty-year-old pilot, she led the crews on the Night of Keys. Now she is
blind and bedridden, but the Council of Keys takes no decision without
her. Every anniversary of the Refusal she dictates a letter to the Smelt
asking permission to lay flowers on Tomas Rell's grave. Every year the
Smelt refuses: officially, Rell is still a deserter.

**Jory of Bell Street**, 27, her great-grandson, commander of the rammers.
Grew up on stories of the cellar. Believes the Tally is a treaty Calder
should never have signed and that the Smelt's mortars must fall silent.
Around him gather **the Cinders** — those who want the Ashborn to stop
choosing and start avenging.

**Ulla of Flour Street**, 36, wing commander, the crew's best pilot. Born
on an airfield, but until twelve she lived in the Fold village of
Loafwell with a foster family: roving life is no life for little ones.
She had a house there, a garden and a dog. Ulla wants the Ashborn to
**settle**: take a weep, hold it to crusting, buy a year's place in the
Ledger's queue, and build a city on the dead ground afterwards — the first
in two hundred years. Around her gather **the Rooted**. Ulla knows where
this year's weep opened. She knows whose village it is.

### THE FOLD — green crew

*"The land bears everything. And remembers everything."*

**Who they are.** The south, the Holdmark's old breadbasket. The Weal
drank the rivers, and where the wheat stood there is now the dry. What
remains are villages along the dead riverbeds — hundreds of them, each
still inhabited, because there is nowhere else to go. The villages form
**the Fold**: each elects a delegate at its **moot**, the delegates elect
the **Fold warden**. The Fold is the most populous crew in the Wealands
and the poorest.

The Fold's banner is green — the colour of fields no one alive has seen.

**What they believe.** That the land is alive. Not in the priest's sense
— in the farmer's: land bears, gives, tires and takes revenge, like any
beast that is treated badly. That the Weal is a wound the northern
engineers dealt the land and the south paid for. When a weep opens in the
Fold, on the first night they throw bread into the crack — not from
superstition, but so the land knows people live here.

**How they fight.** With people. Their barracks are the cheapest of all,
and infantry come out in pairs: **the levy** — village men and women in
heavy quilted vests with knitsteel plates, slow, stubborn, terrifying up
close against other infantry. Fold infantry walk and carry everything on
their backs, which is why they are slow. Fold vehicles are tractors and
combines with bolt-on armour, and they hit structures: if you plough, you
plough. Fold aircraft are **dusters** — light crop-spraying biplanes their
grandfathers flew over the fields for forty years: the fastest wings in
the Wealands, plywood and wire, and they hunt vehicles. Fold towers are
**silos**: grain towers with flak guns on top. The sky over the Fold is a
bad place for other people's planes.

**What they call things.** Hearth — "the board". Wellhead — "the plot".
Energy — "heat". Extractor — "reaper". Tally book — "the audit".

**What they want.** For a weep on their land to go to them.

**What they fear.** That the tallymen find out about **the deep till**.

**The Fold's secret.** Twelve years ago a young Fold agronomist noticed
that weeps open more often where the ground is deeply wounded — at
craters, old quarries, along the heatline trenches. She proposed that the
Fold do what the Harrow did, only small: cut the ground — with old
trenchers, explosives, deep ploughing — in the hope that a small weep
would open where the Fold wanted it. This was called **the deep till**.
The moot voted in secret. For six years nothing came of it. Then it did —
three tiny weeps, a week each, in remote places tallymen never visit. For
the first time in a hundred years the Fold wintered warm.

The Tally says nothing about the deep till — when it was written, nobody
imagined a weep could be caused. But everyone understands: if this gets
out, the other three crews will come to the Fold to cut the ground
themselves. And everyone understands something else: if Ilan Stane was
right about the Spread, the deep till is a knife in that very scar.

**Faces.**

**Prosper Kell**, 63, Fold warden. Careful, cunning, endlessly patient.
Voted for the deep till. For twelve years he has kept it secret from three
crews, from the Ledger and from half his own Fold. He fears two things
most in the world: drought, and a conversation with a tallyman who asks
about quarries.

**Dunne Marrick**, 34, agronomist. The one. Born in the village of
**Magpie Ford**. This spring, forty kilometres from the dead Harrow, by
the ford over the dried-up Magpie, a weep opened — the largest in fifty
years. Right under her village. Dunne does not know whether her deep till
in the next field three years ago caused it. She does not sleep.

**Miron Haye, "Old Duster"**, 71, commander of the duster squadron. Sprayed
fields for forty years, has bombed other crews' extractors for twenty.
Flies without glasses, drinks only on the ground, has never lost a dogfight
over his own village. He says if Magpie Ford burns this year, he will
never fly again.

### THE ROOFERS — yellow crew

*"The Holdmark did not fall. The Holdmark is flooded."*

**Who they are.** The Chancery of Vesk — what remained of the Holdmark's
government after the Drowning. Officials, archivists, ministers' families,
military clerks, teachers, librarians — everyone who didn't get out that
autumn week and climbed to the top floors, then the roofs. There they
stayed. Above the Drown, for two hundred years, stands a city on the
rooftops: gangways between spires, gardens in tin bathtubs, ministries in
the attics of ministries. The crew's official name is the **Chancery of
Vesk**. Everyone else calls them **the Roofers**, and they stopped taking
offence long ago.

Beneath them, under the water, lie the archives of the Holdmark. All of
them.

**What they believe.** That they are the lawful government of the
continent. That the Tally is a temporary arrangement with rebel provinces,
to be revoked as soon as the Holdmark returns. The Roofers still issue
decrees, grant ranks and keep a register of citizens — on paper they dry
on lines strung between the spires. Every crew laughs at this. But only
the Roofers have **divers** who bring the Holdmark's documents up from the
bottom: blueprints, maps, logs, personnel files. Everything the Wealands
know about the Harrow, they know from the Roofers. The Smelt, the Fold and
the Ledger all buy what lies at the bottom of Vesk.

**How they fight.** Fast and without armour. The Roofers are poor: they
have neither the Smelt's factories, nor the Fold's people, nor the
Ashborn's sky. Everything they have is light. Their infantry are divers
and roof-runners, fast, with limpet mines for enemy vehicles. Their
vehicles are **barges** with twin flak guns, slow, but they come off the
slip in pairs. Their aircraft are **kites**: assault gliders launched from
rooftops that drop straight onto structures like gulls onto fish; there
are few roofs to launch from, so their airbase is weak. Their towers hit
infantry. Any hit kills a Roofer: all their gear holds one point of
toughness. So they never stand still.

**What they call things.** Hearth — "the chancery". Wellhead — "the
affected site". Energy — "supply". Tally book — "the minutes". Foundling —
"parcel", and they are the only ones who can sometimes read the address on
it.

**What they want.** A document. Not crystal — crystal keeps them alive —
but the one document they have been looking for for two hundred years.

**What they fear.** That the Holdmark will never come back, and they have
been drying paper for two hundred years for nothing.

**Faces.**

**Seraphine Vale**, 61, Secretary of State of the Chancery. Great-granddaughter
of the Holdmark's last Minister of Transport. Wears a uniform two hundred
years old, re-tailored four times. Cold, precise, superbly educated. The
only crew commander who has read the Tally Accord in the original with the
edits — and knows that the draft of Article Three had one more sentence,
struck out on the last night. Which one, she won't say.

**Tobin Dunn, "Bubble"**, 23, captain of the divers. Born on a roof, never
stood on dry land until sixteen, on a weep for the first time. This winter
he went where no one had gone in two hundred years: into the flooded
basement of the **Traverse Directorate**, thirty-one metres down. He
brought up a crate. In it: the duplicate of the Harrow's watch log, kept
aboard and sent to the capital every week. The last page. Seraphine read
it and ordered Tobin to tell no one what it says. He tells no one. But he
is twenty-three, and he is in love with a cartographer from the Smelt he
met at a siding when the train carried both crews to Magpie Ford.

### The Ledger — not a crew

**Ephram Garth**, 70, trainmaster. Born in car twelve, trainmaster at

## 11. The season at Magpie Ford

The year 212 After the Halt. The forty-seventh weep.

It opened in spring by the ford over the dried-up Magpie, forty kilometres
from the Head. There had not been a weep this large in fifty years:
wellheads burst in several places at once, one of them right at the edge
of the Fold village of Magpie Ford. The Ledger's tallyman declared the
opening six hours later. A week later foundlings began falling on the old
drop rings by the ford. Two weeks later the trains of four crews stood on
the Weal Line.

What each crew brings to this weep, besides an army:

**The Smelt** brings Kessa Stane and her father's notebooks. Kessa needs
three measurements at a wellhead and a little time. If her father was
right, cutting this weep means cutting the scar, and the Council of Heats
will hear it from her. If he was wrong, the Smelt gets its best quota in
ten years, and her mother can sleep.

**The Ashborn** bring a split. The Council of Keys sent both Jory and Ulla
— Mara of Bell Street said: "Let the weep decide." Jory came to tear down
the Smelt's mortars and won't leave until he does. Ulla came to take land,
and cannot take it without doing to Magpie Ford what the Smelt did to
Calder. Her foster family lives in Loafwell, thirty kilometres away.

**The Fold** brings a secret and Dunne Marrick. The Fold has to take this
weep: it is their land and their village. And the Fold has to keep
tallyman Ilse Rane from looking into the quarry on the north edge of the
field.

**The Roofers** bring the last page of the Harrow's log and the boy who
knows what it says. Seraphine Vale did not come for crystal. She came for
the Harrow: the Head is forty kilometres away.

And on the night of the opening, while the tallyman was still on her way
to the field, something happened that only the ledgerfolk and the Roofers
know about yet.

**The Harrow took a step.**

One. Nine metres east. Leg seventeen, sunk in the dry for two hundred
years, came out of the ground roots and all and stood in a new place. The
share-arc, warm for two centuries, glowed that night bright enough to be
seen from the Weal Line. The Ledger wrote it in its log and told no one.
That same week the Provender doubled its drops on the rings at Magpie Ford
— as though the work site had become twice as hot.

The game you play is the season at Magpie Ford. How it ends is up to you.

## 12. What nobody knows

Three questions the Wealands have no answer to. Each crew answers them its
own way, and all four answers may turn out false.

**Why did the Harrow stop?** "Beneath us —" and silence. The Smelt thinks
the heat circuit failed. The Fold says the land bit back. The Ashborn
don't ask. The Roofers know — or think they know: the last page of the
watch log holds an entry made forty minutes before the signal was lost.
Whoever reads it will understand that the Harrow was stopped.

**Where do weeps come from?** The Smelt's official science: the Weal is
venting excess heat. Ilan Stane's paper: a weep is a branch of the scar
that grows faster the more it is cut. The Fold's secret: a weep can be
caused. Rumour in the Wealands: someone is causing them on purpose, and
it isn't the Fold. Because forty-seven weeps in a century and a half is
more than three trenchers could manage.

**What will the Harrow do?** The Harrow is a machine. A machine has no
wishes; it has a program, and the Harrow's last program is to reach the
Eastern Sea. From the Head to the sea is four hundred kilometres. The
straight line from the Head to the sea runs through the Dryfold. Through
Loafwell. Through Magpie Ford.

If cicatrine is the Holdmark's locked heat, then every crate shipped out
of a weep is heat set free again. And if the Harrow stopped because the
heat beneath it ran out — then for two hundred years someone has been
slowly giving it back its fuel.

That someone is everyone.

## 16. What the lore suggests about strategy

The rules don't change with the lore, and any crew can play any strategy.
But each crew has what its character and its troop board pull it toward —
useful for card texts, illustrations and tips for new players.

- **The Smelt — stockpile.** Cheap factories, vehicles in pairs, mortars
  against structures, "process, don't fight". Take wellheads, cover them
  in extractors, fill storage before crusting, hold them off with numbers.
- **The Ashborn — demolition.** Cheap airbases, aircraft in pairs, rammers
  against structures. Don't hold the field; zero out everyone else's:
  burn extractors and storage while others hoard. Maybe the first to
  break Rell.
- **The Fold — presence.** Infantry in pairs, silos against aircraft. Stand
  wherever the charges demand and give up no hexes: slow, but all
  together.
- **The Roofers — knowledge.** Everything fast and everything fragile,
  kites against structures. First to the drop rings, first to the cairns,
  scrapyard and research; live on foundlings and cogs while the others
  fight over crystal.

## 17. The name

**SCARROW.**

A made-up word that sounds like a process — *to scarrow, scarrowing,
scarrowers* — and lives inside the world: it is what the Wealands call
cutting crystal out of a living weep while three other crews try to do
the same. It is built from two words everyone hears in it:

- **scar** — the crystal is scar tissue of the land; the weep is a wound;
  every token is knitsteel that scars over;
- **harrow** — the machine that started it all; a harrow is also the
  farm tool that breaks ground after the plough, and "harrowing" means
  an ordeal.

So the name says what you do at the table — you dig into contested
ground and it hurts everyone — and it is a word from the world rather
than a label on it. One word, seven letters, easy to say, and it reads as
a verb, like *Scythe* reads as a tool.

Possible box line: **"Scarrow — cut the crystal. Hold the ground. Tally
the stars."**

BGG before the name goes on anything.
